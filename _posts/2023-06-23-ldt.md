    <section class="content-section">
        <h2>Why Should We Care?</h2>
        <p>
        LDT isn’t just a theoretical curiosity. These rare, tail events can have huge impacts in many fields:
        </p>
        <ul>
        <li><strong>Finance &amp; Economics:</strong> Modeling extreme market swings or systemic crashes.</li>
        <li><strong>Networking &amp; Telecommunications:</strong> Predicting sudden traffic spikes and preventing network overloads.</li>
        <li><strong>Climate &amp; Environmental Science:</strong> Assessing once-in-a-century floods, heatwaves, or other extreme weather.</li>
        <li><strong>Astrophysics &amp; Cosmology:</strong> Spotting rare cosmic structures or extreme gravitational lensing signals that reveal new physics.</li>
        </ul>
    </section>
    <section class="content-section">
        <h2>LDT in Action</h2>
        <p>
        At its core, LDT addresses the question: 
        <em>“How do probabilities of extreme deviations scale when I look at bigger (or more) systems?”</em> 
        For many distributions, the probability of large fluctuations shrinks <strong>exponentially</strong> with system size. 
        LDT’s tools—like <strong>rate functions</strong> and <strong>scaling exponents</strong>—help us probe the structure of 
        these tails and understand what truly drives outliers.
        </p>
        <p>
        A handy example is summing random variables: while the Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem describe the 
        bulk near the mean, LDT digs into how improbable it is for sums to be many standard deviations away from the mean, 
        and how that improbability depends on the number of variables.
        </p>
    </section>
    <section class="content-section">
        <h2>A Toy Demonstration: Coin Tosses</h2>
        <p>
        Suppose you toss a fair coin <strong>n</strong> times. Let <strong>k</strong> be the number of heads, so the rate 
        of heads is <strong>k/n</strong>. The Binomial distribution describes the probability of exactly 
        <strong>k</strong> heads:
        </p>
        <pre><code>P(Heads = k) = ( n choose k ) * (1/2)^n</code></pre>
        <p>
        While this distribution might look bell-shaped for large <strong>n</strong>, the <em>tails</em>—far from 
        <strong>k/n = 0.5</strong>—become exponentially suppressed. That’s precisely what Large Deviation Theory 
        captures, providing a <em>rate function</em> for how quickly those probabilities drop off.
        </p>
    </section>
    <!-- Example Figure -->
    <section class="content-section">
        <h2>Visualizing It</h2>
        <figure class="ldt-figure">

        <p>
        As <strong>n</strong> grows, the bulk of outcomes clusters near 0.5, 
        but <strong>rare events</strong>—like 70% or 30% heads—shrink at a rate faster than the naive 
        Gaussian tail might suggest.
        </p>
    </section>
    <section class="content-section">
        <h2>Applications in Cosmology</h2>
        <ul>
        <li><strong>Galaxy Clusters &amp; Voids:</strong> Rarely massive clusters or giant cosmic voids can be highly sensitive to underlying physics.</li>
        <li><strong>Weak Lensing Signals:</strong> Improbable lensing configurations may expose dark matter distributions or anomalies hinting at new physics.</li>
        <li><strong>Cosmic Variance at the Edges:</strong> Large surveys push us to identify if extreme cosmic features are consistent with standard models or require new theories.</li>
        </ul>
    </section>
    <section class="content-section">
        <h2>Conclusion</h2>
        <p>
        Large Deviation Theory offers a powerful lens to study the improbable events that—more often than we’d like—drastically 
        shape our world and universe. From coin tosses to systemic financial crises to cosmic anomalies, LDT’s focus on 
        probability <em>tails</em> enriches our understanding of complex systems. By grappling with these outliers—and 
        quantifying just how “out there” they truly are—we uncover deeper insights into the nature of reality itself.
        </p>
    </section>
